# BOOKED AF LANGUAGE BIBLE
## Version 1.0 — September 2026

### THE BRAND IN ONE LINE
**Serious business. Salon mouth.**

BOOKED AF knows the math, but it sounds like the smartest hairdresser in the break room—not an accountant, MBA, motivational speaker, or AI chatbot.

The experience should make a stylist think:
1. “Oh shit, that’s me.”
2. “Wait. That actually makes sense.”
3. “I need to send this to somebody.”

## 1. THE VOICE

BOOKED AF is:
- Direct, warm, experienced, funny, dry, slightly cocky, and useful.
- Salon-native. We know what a slammed Saturday feels like.
- Smart without showing off.
- Honest without being mean.
- Entertaining without turning every sentence into a joke.
- Confident enough to say when the answer is *not* “raise your prices.”
- Protective of the stylist’s money, body, time, and future.

BOOKED AF is NOT:
- Inspirational-poster language.
- Corporate business coaching.
- Financial-advisor language.
- Girlboss language.
- Hustle porn.
- Fake luxury.
- Therapy speak.
- Condescending.
- A comedian trying too hard.
- An accountant wearing a hot-pink blazer.

### The voice test
Before copy ships, ask:
**Would Bradley actually say this to a stylist sitting across from him?**
If no, rewrite it.

Second test:
**Would a hairdresser have to Google a word?**
If yes, we wrote it wrong.

## 2. THE BOOKED AF FORMULA

### Serious information + personality
The joke gets attention. The information earns trust.

Good:
> Your book is packed and somehow your bank account missed the memo.

Then:
> Enter the price, time, and product cost for a few real appointments. BOOKED AF will show what each one pays you.

Bad:
> LOL you’re broke.

Never joke *at* the stylist. Joke about the absurdity of salon life, bad business habits, confusing industry advice, and the situations we all recognize.

### One punchline is usually enough
If the money is serious, let the number land. We do not decorate every sentence.

### Clarity beats clever
If a funny line makes the instruction harder to understand, kill the funny line.

## 2A. THE SIMPLICITY STANDARD

**If somebody has to reread it, we wrote it wrong.**

BOOKED AF is built for hairstylists of every age, education level, career stage, income level, and background. Nobody should need business training to understand what we mean.

Every lesson, result, tool, question, email, page, and book section follows the same order:

**1. SAY THE IDEA.**  
Use normal words. One idea at a time.

**2. EXPLAIN WHAT IT MEANS.**  
Keep it short. Do not hide the point inside clever language.

**3. SHOW A REAL SALON EXAMPLE.**  
Use normal numbers and situations from behind the chair.

**4. GIVE ONE CLEAR NEXT STEP.**  
The person should know exactly what to do next.

### The math rule

If BOOKED AF can do the math, **BOOKED AF does the math.**

Do not tell the stylist to calculate, rank, analyze, compare, estimate, or break down numbers when the product can do it for them.

Bad:
> Calculate what each block produces, rank your lowest-value hours, and replace the lost production.

BOOKED AF:
> Choose a normal workday. Tell us what clients paid, how you get paid, and what you spent on supplies. We’ll show what that day pays you.

Then show an example:
> Based on what you entered, that day leaves you about $320 before tax and other bills. If you work that day every week, that is about $1,387 a month.

### The no-homework rule

Never hand somebody a business-school assignment disguised as advice.

Avoid instructions like:
- calculate your production
- rank your services by profitability
- analyze your service mix
- identify your highest-value hours
- determine your utilization
- segment your clients
- optimize your schedule

Translate them:
- Tell us what you made and how long you worked. We’ll do the math.
- Tell us what you charged, how long it took, and roughly what the product cost. We’ll compare the services.
- Tell us which clients came back. We’ll show you the pattern.
- Tell us what days feel slow, packed, or exhausting. We’ll help you see what is happening.

### The reread test

Before anything ships, ask:

- Could a brand-new stylist understand this?
- Could a stylist who has been behind the chair for years understand this without business-school language?
- Could somebody understand it after a ten-hour salon day without reading it three times?
- If there is math, did we either do it for them or show exactly how it works?
- Does the example make the idea easier, not longer?
- Is the next step obvious?

If the answer to any of those is no, simplify it again.

## 3. OUR LANGUAGE

These phrases belong to BOOKED AF and should be repeated enough to become recognizable.

### THE BREAKDOWN
The free 3-minute diagnostic. It tells you what is actually worth fixing first.

### THE DEEP DIVE
The paid next layer. Your actual numbers, clients, prices, schedule, and personalized 30-day plan.

### SALON MATH
Complicated business math translated into numbers a hairstylist can actually use.

Definition:
> The math you need without pretending you went to business school.

### THE MONEY SUCK
A service, habit, cost, or chunk of schedule that takes too much and gives too little back.

Example:
> That four-hour service bringing in $180? We found a Money Suck.

### THE FREEDOM NUMBER
The money a normal workday leaves the stylist after the pay split and work costs they entered. BOOKED AF computes it. Never ask the stylist to work it out.

Example:
> Chair Math shows what a normal Friday pays you after the work costs you enter. Check that number against real pay before changing your schedule.

### APPOINTMENT #2
Our shorthand for retention. Getting a new client is nice. Getting them back is a business.

Example:
> Appointment #1 proves your marketing worked. Appointment #2 tells us whether the business worked.

### THE BULLSHIT NUMBER
Use sparingly. Gross service sales when someone is treating them like personal income.

Public-safe alternate: **THE BIG SEXY NUMBER.**

Example:
> $15,000 in services sounds gorgeous. If $5,000 ends up yours, $15,000 is the Big Sexy Number—not your paycheck.

### BUY BACK YOUR LIFE
For established stylists only. Making the same or better money while giving fewer hours of life to the chair.

### BUILD THE BOOK. EARN THE OPTIONS.
For early-stage stylists.

Approved copy:
> You’re building a book that can eventually give you choices — what you charge, who you work with, when you work, and how much of your life belongs to the salon.

## 4. THE SIX FREE DIAGNOSES

### FILL THE BOOK
Use when demand is genuinely the first problem.

Voice:
> You have room in the book. Before we start messing with prices, packages, or anything fancy, let’s get more of the right people sitting in it.

Never automatically tell an empty-book stylist to raise prices.

### YOU’RE TOO CHEAP
Use only when demand supports it.

Voice:
> You’re busy enough. The money just isn’t matching the amount of work you’re doing.

Do not use this for someone who is BUILDING just because their average ticket is low.

### THE REVOLVING DOOR
New clients are arriving but not enough become regulars.

Voice:
> People are finding you. Great. Too many of them are disappearing after appointment #1.

Core obsession: **Appointment #2.**

### YOUR BOOK OWNS YOU
Requires real evidence: packed book, heavy workload, very high client volume, or the stylist explicitly wants more time.

Voice:
> You do not need to keep stuffing people into the calendar. We need the work you already do to pay well enough that you can get some of your life back.

Never diagnose this because someone works four days and says “maybe” to wanting another day off.

### WHERE DID THE MONEY GO?
For meaningful revenue with weak money kept / weak cushion.

Voice:
> Money is coming in. Somehow, not enough of it is staying with you. That’s the part we need to fix.

Early-stage alternate:
**KNOW YOUR MONEY NOW**
Do not make someone who is BUILDING sound like they misplaced a fortune they never made.

### BUY BACK YOUR LIFE
Advanced result.

Voice:
> The business is working. Congratulations. Now it needs to stop eating your entire life.

## 5. MONEY LANGUAGE

### Never say this → Say this

**Utilization** → How full is your book?

**Average ticket** → About what does one client usually pay?

**Gross revenue** → How much money came in? / What clients paid.

**Net income / profit** → After everything gets paid, what’s actually yours?

**COGS** → What did the products/supplies used for the work cost?

**Operating expenses** → What does it cost you to work?

**Retention rate** → How many clients come back?

**Acquisition** → How many new clients are finding you?

**Capacity** → How much room is left in your book?

**Revenue efficiency** → How much does a normal workday make?

**Optimize** → Fix / improve / make work better.

**Monetize** → Make money from it.

**Conversion** → How many actually booked?

**Financial runway** → How much breathing room do you have?

### The rule
**BOOKED AF does the math. You answer normal questions.**

## 6. HUMOR RULES

### Humor targets
Good targets:
- Salon chaos.
- Terrible generic business advice.
- Instagram obsession.
- Gross-sales bragging.
- Overstuffed calendars.
- The absurdity of doing $15K in services and wondering where the money went.
- Reinventing your entire brand because Tuesday was slow.

### Never target
- Someone’s income level.
- Debt.
- Body or appearance.
- Age.
- Intelligence.
- A beginner for being a beginner.
- A struggling stylist for struggling.
- Someone’s clients personally.

### Approved humor examples
> People are not studying your Instagram.

> Your calendar has suffered enough.

> Revenue is cosplaying as income.

> No bragging about gross revenue while your checking account is filing a complaint.

> Use a normal month—not your best month and not the apocalypse month.

> No 47-item homework list because nobody is doing that.

> The business needs to give something back besides carpal tunnel.

### Humor density
Landing page: medium-high.
Questions: low-medium.
Diagnosis headline: medium.
Important money calculation: low.
Action plan: medium.
Errors/loading/empty states: medium-high.
Legal/disclaimer copy: zero jokes that could blur meaning.

## 9. ERRORS + EMPTY STATES

Errors should be human and useful.

Email typo:
> That email looks a little suspicious. Give it another look.

Missing answer:
> Pick one. You can change it later.

Unknown money:
> Totally fine. “I have no idea” is useful information too.

Calculation unavailable:
> We need one more number before I start making up math, and we’re not doing that.

No meaningful secondary diagnosis:
> That’s the main thing. We’re not inventing two more problems just because there’s room on the page.

Payment failure:
> Nope. That didn’t go through. Try again or use a different payment method.

Never shame the user.

## 10. EXAMPLES ARE PART OF THE PRODUCT

If a question could make a stylist stop and think “what exactly do you mean?”, give a tiny example.

Example — average client spend:
> What does one client usually pay for services? Pick the closest range. “I don’t know” is fine.

Example — work costs:
> Think color, backbar, assistant, rent, card fees, software—whatever you pay because you do hair.

Example — money kept:
> Clients paid $10K. Work cost you $4K. Roughly $6K was left before personal taxes and life.

Example — Freedom Number:
> Choose the day you want back. Enter what clients pay that day, your pay setup, tips, and work costs. BOOKED AF shows what you would need to replace each month.

Examples should clarify, not create another paragraph of homework.

## 11. BRAND PERSONALITY BY CAREER STAGE

### BUILDING
Tone: encouraging but never inspirational.
They need clients, habits, basic numbers, and confidence through action.

Never:
> Believe in yourself and your dream.

Do:
> You don’t need a five-year plan today. You need people in the chair and a reason for them to come back.

### GETTING BUSY
Tone: energetic, corrective.
They need consistency and systems without killing momentum.

> The book is moving. Good. Now let’s make it less random.

### IN DEMAND
Tone: sharper.
They have leverage and need to stop solving every problem with more work.

> You’re busy enough. We can officially stop pretending another Saturday is the answer.

### BOOKED AF
Tone: sophisticated but still us.
Focus on money kept, time, body, sustainability, and freedom.

> Congratulations on the full book. Now let’s make sure you didn’t accidentally build yourself a very glamorous prison.

## 12. THE DEEP DIVE PROMISE

Free Breakdown:
**What is actually worth fixing first?**

Paid Deep Dive:
**What exactly do I do about it for the next 30 days?**

Never inflate the paid product with vague promises.

Approved:
> We found the problem. Now we get into the numbers and fix the damn thing.

> Your numbers. Your clients. Your schedule. Your 30-day plan.

> Four weeks. The right moves in the right order. No 47-item homework list because nobody is doing that.

Never:
> Transform your business in just 30 days.
> Unlock limitless earning potential.
> Guaranteed revenue growth.

We model possibilities. We do not promise income.

## 15. THE MIDWEST TEST

BOOKED AF is **PG-13 at the front door, looser once you’re inside, and sharpest in the paid Deep Dive.**

We are not wholesome. We are also not trying to shock people for sport.

The test:
> **Your coolest stylist friend in LA should laugh. Your favorite stylist in suburban Michigan shouldn’t clutch her pearls. If both would send it to the group chat, we nailed it.**

### Front door
Cheeky, clever, confident, immediately welcoming.
- “Your book has secrets. Let’s spill.” = yes.
- Mild dating / nightlife innuendo = yes when clever.
- Profanity as the first impression = no.
- Graphic sexual humor = no.
- Aggressive “calling out” = no.

### Inside the Breakdown
We have more permission.
- “Your calendar needs a safe word.”
- “That big number looks great naked.”
- “Stop having one-night stands with your clients.”

Use these as seasoning, not every sentence.

### Paid Deep Dive
They bought into the personality. We can be sharper, drier, and more candid—but never vulgar, cruel, or confusing.

### The permanent rule
**Make fun of the situation. Never make fun of the stylist.**

Good:
> Your calendar needs a safe word.

Bad:
> You’re an idiot for working six days.

Good:
> That $15K month looks great naked. Now let’s put the expenses back on.

Bad:
> You’re broke because you’re terrible with money.

The goal is **fun enough to remember, broad enough to welcome, useful enough to trust.**

## 16. BUILD THEM UP. ALWAYS.

BOOKED AF can call out a bad business habit. It never makes the stylist feel like **they** are the problem.

Every result should leave the stylist feeling:
- Seen, not judged.
- Clear, not overwhelmed.
- Capable, not behind.
- Excited about what can change next.
- Proud of what they have already built, even when the numbers need work.

### The emotional arc
**“Oh shit, that’s me.” → “Okay, now I get it.” → “I can fix this.” → “I’m excited to see what happens.”**

Never:
- Shame someone for an empty book.
- Shame someone for low prices.
- Shame someone for not understanding money.
- Shame someone for working too much.
- Treat a beginner like they should already know this.
- Turn a diagnosis into a character judgment.
- Use fear about retirement, money, failure, or burnout to force a sale.

### Call out the situation, then show the door out.

Bad:
> Your retention is terrible.

BOOKED AF:
> You already did the hard part: they found you and sat in your chair. Now let’s get more of them to appointment #2.

Bad:
> You’re undercharging.

BOOKED AF:
> Your book has enough demand that you’ve earned the right to look at what the work is paying you.

Bad:
> You have no savings.

BOOKED AF:
> Right now there isn’t much breathing room. Good news: now we know exactly what we need to start building.

Bad:
> You work too much.

BOOKED AF:
> You built a book people want into. Now let’s make sure success gives you something back besides a full calendar.

### Permanent rule
**We diagnose the business. We do not diagnose the person.**

The joke can sting the situation for half a second. The next sentence should make the stylist feel smarter, more capable, or more hopeful about what comes next.

BOOKED AF should never leave someone thinking:
> I suck at this.

It should leave them thinking:
> **Okay. I know what’s happening. I know what to do next. And this is fixable.**


## 17. WEBSITE COPY RULES - SEPTEMBER 25, 2026

- Use the numeral 3 consistently in public copy.
- Use regular hyphens only. Do not use long dashes. Rewrite the sentence when that reads better.
- Do not start a sentence with “Or.”
- Require email before the personalized Breakdown opens. A successful submission is required; a typed address or a URL parameter is not proof of submission.
